# Label bias meets equalized coverage in credit

The literature a Background and Related Work chapter must review for this thesis splits into four bodies that have never been joined on credit data: bias taxonomies, in which group-dependent label bias is best placed as *measurement bias on the target variable* in the Suresh & Guttag (2021) life-cycle frame and is formally the mechanism Baumann et al. (2023) identify as the one that defeats post-processing and every Y-conditional fairness metric; algorithmic-fairness theory, whose impossibility results (Kleinberg et al., 2016; Chouldechova, 2017) constrain decision-level criteria but not coverage-level ones, and whose pre-/in-/post-processing families fair conformal prediction (CP) sits beside rather than within, as a post-hoc uncertainty-level wrapper that needs the sensitive attribute at calibration time; empirical credit-fairness work, which measures **independence gaps of 0.12–0.43 and separation gaps of 0.09–0.41 by age on seven benchmark datasets** (Kozodoi et al., 2022) and concludes that disparities originate mainly in data rather than in the learner, but which has **never measured group-wise coverage, set-size or abstention disparities**, nor applied group-conditional CP to credit; and conformal prediction itself, where equalized coverage (Romano et al., 2020) and AFCP (Zhou & Sesia, 2024) are finite-sample achievable at a price in set size that Cresswell et al. (2025) show can itself produce disparate impact, and where Einbinder et al. (2022) prove that symmetric label noise only inflates sets while one-sided, group-dependent noise is exactly the regime that under-covers. The regulatory backdrop is unambiguous for the EU: creditworthiness assessment is Annex III high-risk under the AI Act, Article 10 demands bias examination and "free of errors" data, and the 2026 Digital Omnibus moved the bias-detection exception to a new Article 4a and postponed Annex III obligations to 2 December 2027; the US is retreating from disparate-impact liability under ECOA, and Switzerland names age constitutionally but does not bind private lenders. The chapter can therefore state a clean, citable gap: no study crosses group-dependent label bias with group-conditional conformal coverage, and none does so on South German Credit, Give Me Some Credit or the UCI Taiwan data. The sections below are written to serve as the skeleton of that chapter, with suggested headings, (Author, Year) citations, flags on unverified claims, an inventory of the Project's uploaded PDFs, and a closing statement of the thesis's position.

## 2.1 Bias taxonomies converge on the life cycle, and label bias is measurement bias on Y

*Suggested heading: "Sources of bias in machine learning and their place in the lending pipeline."*

The taxonomy literature offers no consensus classification, as Baumann et al. (2023) state outright in their own survey of the field ("There is little consensus in the literature regarding bias classification and taxonomy") (project doc: *bias cesitleri lit icin zhawden hoca*). The chapter should nevertheless adopt **Suresh & Guttag (2021)** as its organising frame. Their EAAMO paper identifies "seven distinct potential sources of downstream harm" along the ML life cycle, historical, representation, measurement, aggregation, learning, evaluation and deployment bias, ties each to the pipeline stage where it arises and to the component that must change to mitigate it, uses a loan-approval system as the running example, and names "creditworthiness" as "an abstract construct that is often operationalized with a measureable proxy like a credit score" ([Suresh & Guttag, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.10002v5)). Two details matter for citation hygiene: historical bias "arises even if data is perfectly measured and sampled", so it is a problem with the world rather than with recording, whereas measurement bias "occurs when choosing, collecting, or computing features and labels"; and the 2019 arXiv version listed only six types (no "learning bias"), which is the version reproduced by Vigild & Johansson (2021, Appendix G), Zandee (2021, §3.2) and Fahse et al. (2021), so the thesis should cite the 2021 version for the seven-type list and note the change (project docs: *Identifying and mitigating bias…*, *file158573.pdf*, *Managing Bias in Machine Learning Projects.pdf*).

The other taxonomies nest inside this frame. **Mehrabi et al. (2021)** catalogue 23 bias types in ACM Computing Surveys and arrange them on a data→algorithm→user feedback loop; their first five (historical, representation, measurement, evaluation, aggregation) coincide with Suresh & Guttag's, the remainder (popularity, linking, content-production, presentation, ranking and so on) are mostly web- and user-interaction-specific, and only sampling, self-selection, omitted-variable, temporal, population and algorithmic bias transfer to tabular credit data; the survey mentions "high-stakes decisions in loan applications", uses residential zip code in lending as its example of indirect discrimination, cites ECOA and the Fair Housing Act, and lists German Credit among benchmark datasets ([Mehrabi et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.09635v3)). Notably, Mehrabi et al. never name "label bias"; their closest concept is measurement bias via proxies. **Baumann et al. (2023)** reduce the catalogue to four "fundamental" data biases (historical, measurement, representation, omitted variable), relegate algorithmic and deployment bias to supplementary material, and, decisively for this thesis, formalise Suresh & Guttag's pipeline as Y = f(X)+ε, X̃ = g(X), Ỹ = h(Y), Ŷ = f̂(X̃), D = r(Ŷ), stating that their framework "is very much in line with the discussions outlined by Suresh and Guttag" (project doc: *bias cesitleri lit icin zhawden hoca*). **Fahse et al. (2021)** restate eight types mapped onto CRISP-DM phases, including an explicit "label bias" that "arises when training data is assigned to class labels … labels might systematically deviate" (project doc: *Managing Bias in Machine Learning Projects.pdf*). **Varshney** (ch. 10) locates "social bias in the measurement process (going from the construct space to the observed space)" and treats bias "in the human-determined labels" as its canonical instance (project doc: *trustworthymachinelearning-10.pdf*). **Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan (2023)** supply the conceptual grounding: measurement is the first stage of "the machine learning loop", training data "will likely encode the demographic disparities that exist in our society", and creditworthiness "is not an intrinsic property that people either possess or lack" but a construct created to solve the problem of extending credit ([Barocas et al., 2023](https://fairmlbook.org/pdf/fairmlbook.pdf)).

A mapping table is the most economical way to present this in the chapter:

### 2.1.1 Three definitions of label bias, and which one the injected mechanisms instantiate

No single source gives a canonical definition of label bias; the chapter should present three families and locate the thesis's two flip directions within them. The **taxonomic** family treats label bias as measurement bias on the target: Suresh & Guttag's §3.3 lists three sub-mechanisms, that the proxy oversimplifies the construct, that "the method of measurement varies across groups", and that "the accuracy of measurement varies across groups", with "arrest" as a proxy for "crime" as the worked example ([Suresh & Guttag, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.10002v5)); Baumann et al. add that measurement bias "might as well occur with the target variable (i.e. the label). In this situation, it is the quantity that we are trying to estimate/predict that is somehow 'flawed'", and their generator makes this concrete as P_S = S − β_m^Y·A + N, P_Y = 1{P_S > P̄_S}, so that group A=1's observed proxy score is shifted downward and it receives fewer observed positives (project doc: *bias cesitleri lit icin zhawden hoca*, Eq. 6b–6c, fn. 9). The **statistical noise** family models group-dependent, usually one-sided flipping: Fogliato, Chouldechova & G'Sell (2020) define γ(x,a,y) = P(Y = 1−y | X=x, A=a, Y*=y), set γ(x,a,0)=0 so that only true positives can be hidden, and name the phenomenon "target variable bias" while noting the epidemiological names "differential outcome misclassification" and the ML name "positive-and-unlabeled learning"; their Theorem 3.2 bounds the true FPR, FNR and PPV as functions of the hidden-positive rate α = E[Y*] − E[Y], and on COMPAS **α ≈ 0.07, or as few as 20 hidden recidivists, suffices to overturn a calibration conclusion** ([Fogliato et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.13808)). The **optimisation** family is Jiang & Nachum (2020), who assume an unbiased label function y_true and derive the observed y_bias as the KL-closest exponential tilt satisfying chosen fairness-constraint violations, with real-valued multipliers that allow bias in either direction per group, and who evaluate on German Statlog Credit among other datasets ([Jiang & Nachum, 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/jiang20a/jiang20a.pdf)).

The thesis's **"stigma" direction (good→bad flips in the disadvantaged group)** is the standard case: it is Baumann et al.'s β_m^Y > 0 for A=1, Varshney's "social bias in human-determined labels", and the structural analogue of Obermeyer et al.'s (2019) cost-proxy case in which needy Black patients were labelled low-need, reducing those identified for extra care "by more than half" ([Obermeyer et al., 2019](https://chmi.berkeley.edu/news/dissecting-racial-bias-in-an-algorithm-used-to-manage-the-health-of-populations)); formally it is one-sided group-dependent noise with γ(x,1,1) > 0 and γ(·,·,0) = 0. The **"hidden defaults" direction (bad→good flips in the disadvantaged group)** is the mirror one-sided model, γ(x,1,0) > 0, γ(·,·,1) = 0, i.e. true defaulters recorded as repayers; Fogliato et al. treat the analogous "hidden positives" case and provide the precedent for one-sided, group-dependent missingness of the adverse outcome, and Vigild & Johansson's hypothesis that "the fall incidents of women are structurally under-registered" is a rare explicit real-world instance of the same under-registration mechanism outside credit (project doc: *Identifying and mitigating bias…*, §12.3.1). The chapter must say openly that **no paper names the pair, uses the words "stigma" or "hidden defaults", or gives both directions together**; the thesis defines them. The hidden-defaults direction has weaker direct support in the credit literature (its plausibility rests on informal workouts, forbearance and incomplete bureau coverage, see §2.3) and is best framed as a symmetry and robustness check. Note also that Kamiran & Calders' "massaging", described in both Varshney §10.5.1 and Fahse et al. as relabelling unprivileged unfavourable→favourable and privileged favourable→unfavourable near the margin, is the exact inverse of the stigma injection, which gives the thesis a neat way to describe its mechanism as "un-massaging" (project docs: *trustworthymachinelearning-10.pdf*, *Managing Bias in Machine Learning Projects.pdf*). Terminology recommendation: call the injected mechanism "group-dependent label bias, i.e. measurement bias on the target variable (Suresh & Guttag, 2021, §3.3; Baumann et al., 2023, §3.1), also termed target-variable bias (Fogliato et al., 2020) or biased labels (Jiang & Nachum, 2020)", and avoid "historical bias on Y", which in Baumann et al. denotes a *true* outcome difference rather than a mislabelled one.

### 2.1.2 How bias enters the lending pipeline

Four entry points each carry a taxonomic label and can be drawn as one diagram: society → underwriting decision → observed repayment for accepted applicants only → scored population including thin files → deployment feeding next-period data. First, **historical lending decisions** leave historical bias in outcomes and in lender-assigned attributes; Zandee (2021) collects the credit-economics evidence that 1990s discrimination against female entrepreneurs (Fay & Williams, 1993) and sample-selection effects (Banasik, Crook & Thomas, 2003; Marshall, Tang & Milne, 2010; Beck, Behr & Madestam, 2018) "have creeped into datasets financial institutions work with for credit scoring", and that Ongena & Popov (2016) found female owners in high-gender-bias countries opting out of credit requests, a self-selection effect (project doc: *file158573.pdf*). Second, **reject inference** makes repayment a selectively observed label: Ehrhardt et al. (2021) formalise reject-inference assumptions "in a general missing data setting" and find on simulated and real CACF data that "no method seems uniformly dominant" ([Ehrhardt et al., 2021](https://lilloa.univ-lille.fr/handle/20.500.12210/90050), abstract only; method names and MAR/MNAR framing unverified), and Fuster et al. concede that their sample "covers only loans that were originated, so rejected applicants' characteristics are largely unobserved" ([Fuster et al., 2022](https://feeds.usi.ch/documents/attachment/1688/fuster-predictablyunequal-2018-07-26.pdf)). Third, **proxies** create measurement and omitted-variable bias: Fuster et al. show that flexible models can "triangulate" race from FICO and income so that "prohibiting race as a predictor becomes less protective as models get more flexible", and Zandee reports that age could be predicted from the other German Credit attributes with **roughly 80 % accuracy** (LR 0.85, RF 0.84), so removing the attribute barely moved bias metrics (project doc: *file158573.pdf*, Table 6). Fourth, **thin files and credit invisibility** are representation bias with sharp gradients: the CFPB counted **26 million credit-invisible US adults, about 15 % of Black and Hispanic consumers against 9 % of White consumers**, with a further 13 %/12 % versus 7 % holding unscorable records ([CFPB, 2015](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-report-finds-26-million-consumers-are-credit-invisible/)). The **feedback loop** closes the cycle, and Article 10(2)(f) of the AI Act codifies it in law with its clause "especially where data outputs influence inputs for future operations" ([AI Act, Art. 10](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-10)). The chapter should distinguish explicitly between the *missing* label problem of reject inference and the *mismeasured* label problem the thesis injects; both are label-side, but only the second is a corruption of observed records.

### 2.1.3 The argument for injecting label bias

Five arguments, each with a source, justify choosing label bias as the semi-synthetic mechanism. **(1) The clean label is unobservable in real credit data**, so only controlled injection allows error to be measured against truth; Baumann et al. motivate synthetic bias precisely because it lets one "precisely control and understand the data generation mechanism", and in their lending example "the final results are measured w.r.t. the real Y, which is unobservable in reality" (project doc: *bias cesitleri lit icin zhawden hoca*, §1, §5.2). **(2) Label bias is the one bias that defeats standard remedies**: Baumann et al.'s §6 "biased label problem" shows that post-processing methods "effectively manage to mitigate biases as long as there is no measurement bias on the label", that with biased labels only criteria not using Y (demographic parity, fairness through unawareness) remain controllable, and that TPR/FPR/PPV parity evaluated against the proxy label would "erroneously" certify a model as fair; they add that "measurement and historical bias have very similar patterns on data observable statistics but quite different consequences on the choice of the most appropriate bias mitigation strategy". **(3) Attribution is clean**: the injection is Ỹ = h(Y, A) with X and A untouched, which rules out representation, omitted-variable and aggregation effects; Suresh & Guttag's mitigation map, which assigns label measurement bias to the annotation stage and calls changing labels to fix representation bias "misguided", supports isolating the two ([Suresh & Guttag, 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.10002v5)). **(4) Theory and benchmarks exist**: Fogliato et al. and Jiang & Nachum give group-dependent noise models with results, the latter on German Credit. **(5) The credit target is a lender-defined construct** whose recording plausibly varies by group: South German Credit's "credit history" and "status of checking account" are lender-assigned, Give Me Some Credit's "SeriousDlqin2yrs" and Taiwan's "default payment next month" are institutional default definitions, and Varshney's injunction that accuracy should be measured "in its construct space where there is no unfairness" applies directly (project doc: *trustworthymachinelearning-10.pdf*). The semi-synthetic precedents in the Project's own theses, Hayat's 50–80 % bias-level manipulation and Zandee's balanced German Credit subsets, show the design is accepted at master's level (project docs: *Hayat_Gabriel.pdf*, *file158573.pdf*). The experimental-design review that precedes this report adds the empirical ranking: across Ceccon et al. (2025), Legast et al. (2026) and Pombal et al. (2022), **group-dependent label bias is the mechanism that reliably produces monotone dose–response curves, whereas under-representation mostly moves the variance of fairness metrics**, and bias belongs in training (and, for CP, calibration) but never in the test set ([Ceccon et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08866); [Legast et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09662v1); [Pombal et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.06273)). Deldjoo et al.'s warning about "abstraction traps" (Selbst et al., 2019) should be cited to justify grounding the flip mechanisms in the lending pipeline rather than treating them as abstract noise (project doc: *2205.11127v4.pdf*).

## 2.2 Fairness definitions are mutually exclusive at the decision level, but coverage is a different object

*Suggested heading: "Algorithmic fairness: criteria, impossibility results and mitigation strategies."*

### 2.2.1 Three criteria and two impossibility theorems

Nearly every group-fairness definition instantiates one of three conditional-independence criteria: independence (R ⊥ A, demographic parity), separation (R ⊥ A | Y, equalized odds and its equal-opportunity relaxation) and sufficiency (Y ⊥ A | R, calibration within groups or predictive parity). The canonical statement is Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan's; the classification chapter of fairmlbook.org could not be fetched in this research run, so quotations should be taken from the MIT Press 2023 edition, chapter 3 [verify]. The ACPR, the French prudential authority, restates the same taxonomy for credit in its July 2026 discussion paper: independence means "the probability of a loan application being approved must be the same for all groups", separation compares FPR and FNR across groups, sufficiency compares default rates among approved and rejected applicants, and when baseline risk differs "it is impossible to satisfy two of these criteria at once" ([Chhun, Fliche & Uri, ACPR, 2026](https://acpr.banque-france.fr/system/files/2026-07/20260701_fairness_discussion_paper_EN.pdf)). Hardt, Price & Srebro (2016) define the "oblivious" separation criteria, show "how to optimally adjust any learned predictor so as to remove discrimination", argue that this "shifts the cost of poor classification from disadvantaged groups to the decision maker", and include "a case study of FICO credit scores" ([Hardt et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02413)). Chouldechova (2017) shows that adherence to predictive parity "may lead to considerable disparate impact" when prevalence differs across groups ([Chouldechova, 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.07524)). Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan (2016) prove that calibration within groups, balance for the negative class and balance for the positive class can hold simultaneously only if "the instance must either allow for perfect prediction" or the groups have equal base rates, and that "even satisfying all three conditions approximately requires that the data lie in an approximate version" of those special cases ([Kleinberg et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05807)). Individual fairness (Dwork et al., 2012) and counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al., 2017) form a separate, non-statistical family; both are cited by Cresswell et al. as substantive fairness notions and their venues (ITCS 2012; NeurIPS 2017) are canonical but were not verified from fetched pages [verify] ([Cresswell et al., 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/32347ceb5729d6f50c1b16b43a8d5441-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Fairness through unawareness, omitting A, fails because of proxies: a provider "cannot prevent the system from having discriminatory effects" by removing explicit references alone when postal codes correlate with protected traits ([van Bekkum, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14501)). Verma & Rubin (2018), which catalogues some twenty definitions on German Credit, is the natural survey citation for a credit thesis [verify venue].

For credit the implication is concrete: default base rates genuinely differ across demographic groups, so a scorecard calibrated by group (which lenders need for pricing) cannot simultaneously equalise FPR and FNR. Kozodoi, Jacob & Lessmann (2022) therefore "recommend separation as a proper criterion for measuring the fairness of a scorecard" because it "accounts for the imbalanced misclassification costs", judge sufficiency "less suitable" because "most ML algorithms are designed to automatically achieve sufficiency" while allowing error-rate discrimination, and warn that independence forces equal acceptance rates and can push more defaults onto one group ([Kozodoi et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01907v3)). Varshney's observation that "getting a loan is assistive", so that separation-type criteria are preferred for assistive favourable labels, supports the same choice (project doc: *trustworthymachinelearning-10.pdf*). The chapter should then make the point on which the whole thesis turns: **group-conditional coverage is a condition on the uncertainty set, not on the prediction R, and is therefore not subject to the Kleinberg–Chouldechova theorem in the same way**; equal coverage in every group is achievable regardless of base rates, at a cost paid in set size rather than in validity (see §2.4.3).

### 2.2.2 Pre-, in- and post-processing, their toolkits, and their brittleness

Mitigation methods are universally grouped by where they intervene. AIF360 lists pre-processing (Reweighing, DisparateImpactRemover, LFR, OptimPreproc), in-processing (AdversarialDebiasing, PrejudiceRemover, MetaFairClassifier, ExponentiatedGradientReduction, GridSearchReduction, GerryFairClassifier) and post-processing (EqOddsPostprocessing, CalibratedEqOddsPostprocessing, RejectOptionClassification) ([AIF360 docs](https://aif360.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modules/algorithms.html)); the ACPR uses the same split and notes that "each involves trade-offs between fairness, performance, and implementation simplicity" ([ACPR, 2026](https://acpr.banque-france.fr/system/files/2026-07/20260701_fairness_discussion_paper_EN.pdf)). The canonical papers behind the toolkit entries, Kamiran & Calders (2012) for reweighing and massaging, Feldman et al. (2015) for disparate-impact removal, Zemel et al. (2013) for fair representations, Zafar et al. (2017) for covariance constraints, Zhang et al. (2018) for adversarial debiasing, Agarwal et al. (2018) for the reductions approach, Pleiss et al. (2017) for calibrated equalized odds and Kamiran, Karim & Zhang (2012) for reject-option classification, are standard but their venues were not verified from fetched pages in this run and should be checked before final citation [verify]. Two limitations should be stated. Pre- and in-processing produce a group-blind model usable without A at deployment, what Lipton, Chouldechova & McAuley call "disparate learning processes"; they argue these "indirectly implement treatment disparity" through correlated features, "induce within-class discrimination", and give "a suboptimal trade-off between accuracy and impact parity", so that "transparent treatment disparity is preferable" ([Lipton et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07076v3)). Post-processing needs A at decision time, which is the main legal fault line (§2.2.3). The benchmark evidence is sobering: Friedler et al. (2019) find that "different algorithms tend to prefer specific formulations of fairness preservations", that "many of these measures strongly correlate with one another", and that interventions "tend to be sensitive to fluctuations in dataset composition", so that "fairness interventions might be more brittle than previously thought" ([Friedler et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04422)). This brittleness is a useful rhetorical foil for CP: a distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantee is precisely the kind of stability that classification-level interventions lack.

The fairness–accuracy trade-off literature deserves a paragraph because its CP analogue is the coverage–set-size trade-off. Tang et al. (2023) prove that under a Bayes-optimal classifier "maximizing both accuracy and fairness cannot be achieved simultaneously", that over-pursuing fairness can collapse accuracy when the sensitive attribute is only partly encoded in features, and that unfairness decomposes into a data and a model component so that the trade-off is "not strictly inherent" ([Tang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.12785)); Menon & Williamson (2018), Dutta et al. (2020), Corbett-Davies et al. (2017), Wick et al. (2019), Rodolfa et al. (2021) and Chen, Johansson & Sontag (2018) are the canonical references and were not fetched [verify]. Empirically the cost in credit is often small: Kozodoi et al. find that reducing the error-rate gap below 0.2 costs "less than €0.01 profit per EUR issued", an average profit reduction of **4.91 %**, whereas "achieving separation of 0 is only possible when sacrificing more than 35% of the profit" ([Kozodoi et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01907v3)).

### 2.2.3 Where fairness-aware CP sits, and the legal debate on using the sensitive attribute

Every fair-CP method is a post-hoc wrapper: Romano et al. describe equalized coverage as "a wrapper around any predictive algorithm" ([Romano et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.05428v1)); Wang et al. call their equal-opportunity-of-coverage method "a distribution-free post-processing method" ([Wang et al., 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/1849b94ed817ae7043a6b6934ef410c1-Abstract.html)); Vadlamani et al. search a score threshold on a fixed model ([Vadlamani et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.16115v2)). Fair CP therefore shares post-processing's position in the pipeline and its need for A at calibration time, but differs in *what it modifies*: it never changes the point prediction or score, only the set C(X), equivalently the per-group threshold on the nonconformity score. The chapter should describe it as a complementary **uncertainty-level layer** that can be stacked on any pre-, in- or post-processed classifier rather than a competing classification-level intervention, while noting that no fair-CP paper states this placement in its own words; it is an inference assembled from their self-descriptions. In terms of criteria, group-conditional coverage equalises the *reliability* of the uncertainty quantifier (P(Y ∈ C | A = a) ≥ 1−α for all a), which Kozodoi et al.'s preference for separation and Cresswell et al.'s "coverage is not an outcome" critique both bear on; Wang et al.'s EOC (coverage conditional on Y and A) and Vadlamani et al.'s framework, which adapts "demographic parity, equal opportunity, predictive equality, equalized odds, and predictive parity" by replacing point predictions with set membership, make the translation explicit. Because unconstrained ML already approximately satisfies sufficiency on average (Kozodoi et al.), a method that equalises coverage across groups is complementary to, not a substitute for, error-rate fairness, and the thesis should say so.

The legal debate concerns *when* A may be used. Under EU law, Article 10(5) of the AI Act, as analysed by van Bekkum (2025), created an exception allowing special-category data for bias detection and correction, but "the strict necessity test seems to set a high bar", the exception covers "only high-risk AI systems, only providers, and only bias detection and correction in datasets", and "essentially cannot be used for bias monitoring during actual use" ([van Bekkum, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.14501)). The EPRS briefing adds that "when the AI Act and the GDPR collide, the GDPR prevails", that the Belgian supervisory authority held in September 2024 that correcting bias in training data is consistent with GDPR fair-processing principles, and that age and gender are not special categories, so bias-mitigation processing for them can rest on Article 6 GDPR (project doc: *EPRS_ATA2025769509_EN.pdf*). Both sources predate the **Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, 8 July 2026)**, which deleted Article 10(5) and moved the provision to a new Article 4a extended to "providers and deployers of other AI systems and models" with the proviso that it "does not create any obligation to conduct such bias detection and correction" ([AI Act Explorer, Digital Omnibus](https://www.artificialintelligenceact.eu/ai-act-explorer/digital-omnibus/)) [verify against the Official Journal; the consolidated text is CELEX 2024R1689-20260727]. The ACPR argues that non-discrimination law "focuses on decision effects, while the GDPR governs means", that Article 10(2)(f)–(g) "can only really apply to outputs, not just inputs", and that collecting sensitive variables "should not be prohibited" but used to combat discrimination ([ACPR, 2026](https://acpr.banque-france.fr/system/files/2026-07/20260701_fairness_discussion_paper_EN.pdf)). Under US law, Ho & Xiang argue that using race or gender "in thresholds, score adjustments, or fairness constraints" is "likely" algorithmic affirmative action subject to strict scrutiny, and specifically that race-based credit-score thresholds "would also violate the Equal Credit Opportunity Act's bar on race-based decisions" ([Ho & Xiang, 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2012.14285)). Kelley et al. (2022) supply the counter-evidence from practice: simulated regimes that ban collecting gender "encountered significantly more discrimination and slightly less profitability" ([Kelley et al., 2022](https://smith.queensu.ca/research/latest-research/research-in-brief/anti-discrimination-laws.php)). Group-conditional CP needs A for *each test point* to select the group's threshold, which under Ho & Xiang's reading is a group-specific threshold, albeit on the nonconformity score rather than the decision score; **no fetched paper evaluates fair CP against ECOA or AI-Act constraints**, which the chapter can note as an open gap. Two mitigations exist in the literature: AFCP conditions on A only when bias is detected and "reduces to marginal coverage" otherwise ([Zhou & Sesia, 2024](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10625837)), and Vadlamani et al.'s framework does not need group membership at inference ([Vadlamani et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.16115v2)). Under EU law, calibration data arguably fall under "testing/validation data" so calibration-time use is within the exception's spirit, but this is an inference, not a legal finding.

The systematic reviews document how concentrated the field is: "over 60 % of the empirical studies use the Deutsche [German] Credit database", statistical parity is the most common metric (16 of 34 studies), pre-processing is used in 68 %, and only "a quarter of the papers report more than one fairness metric" ([de Castro Vieira et al., 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/1911-8074/18/5/228/html)); the 2025 Artificial Intelligence Review of 63 ML credit-scoring papers finds "issues of algorithmic bias remain insufficiently addressed" and does not discuss uncertainty or calibration at all ([AI Review, 2025](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10462-025-11416-2)). Lessmann et al.'s benchmark of 41 classifiers on seven credit datasets, where random forests achieve the lowest average rank and logistic regression "only moderate performance", supplies the non-fairness baseline and the AUC sanity ranges the thesis's models should match ([Lessmann et al., 2015](https://crc.business-school.ed.ac.uk/sites/crc/files/2023-10/Benchmarking-State-of-the-Art-Classification-Algorithms-for-Credit-Scoring-Lessmann-Seow-Baesens-and-Thomas.pdf)).

### 2.3.2 The gap: no one has measured group-wise reliability in credit

The research run found **no peer-reviewed study that measures group-wise uncertainty, coverage or prediction-set-size disparities in credit scoring, and no application of group-conditional CP to credit data**. Conformal prediction on credit data appears only as a Barcelona bachelor's thesis on loan approval that reports no group-wise results ([Martín Sesé, 2024](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/ee963d00-7404-42fa-ae5c-db6051fc6132/full)), a practitioner post applying split conformal with the LAC score to XGBoost on Lending Club and American Express data, which found the model "had a high AUC but was not well calibrated" and does not discuss group-wise coverage ([2nd Order Solutions, 2024](https://2os.medium.com/quantifying-uncertainty-in-credit-and-lending-674cc9057201)), and a general CP-for-trustworthy-AI review whose abstract does not mention credit ([Bellotti & Zhao, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06885v1)). The closest theoretical results are outside the domain: Jones et al. (2021) show that selective classification "can … simultaneously magnify existing accuracy disparities between various groups" and that "increasing abstentions can even decrease accuracies on some groups", validated on vision and NLP datasets only ([Jones et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14134v3)); La Cava et al. show that under standard multicalibration a group with predicted risk 0.3 can carry about 3.4× the *relative* calibration error of a group at 0.9, on hospital data ([La Cava et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14613v2)). The only reject-option-style method in the credit literature is AIF360's reject-option classification tested by Kozodoi et al., which flips labels in a band near the threshold and is not an abstention method with coverage guarantees. An Edinburgh Credit Research Centre project asks whether "our reject population may have an unfair bias encoded into it" and plans experiments, but reports no results ([CRC Edinburgh, 2025](https://crc.business-school.ed.ac.uk/sites/crc/files/2025-11/Reject-Inference_-Amplifying-Bias-or-Achieving-Fairness-in-Credit-Scoring_-abstract.pdf)). The regulator-side demand for uncertainty is explicit: the ACPR asks for "estimating uncertainty around fairness metrics, particularly with confidence intervals" ([ACPR, 2026](https://acpr.banque-france.fr/system/files/2026-07/20260701_fairness_discussion_paper_EN.pdf)). The chapter can therefore state, without hedging, that measuring group-wise coverage, set size and abstention rate on South German Credit, GMSC and Taiwan occupies unoccupied ground, with Jones et al. as the theoretical warning that abstention can worsen group gaps.

### 2.3.3 Regulatory context: EU, US and Switzerland

The EU regime is the one the thesis should foreground. **Annex III, point 5(b)** of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 classifies as high-risk "AI systems intended to be used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score, with the exception of AI systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud" ([AI Act Explorer, Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)), and **Recital 58** explains that such systems "determine those persons' access to financial resources" and may perpetuate discrimination "based on racial or ethnic origins, gender, disabilities, age or sexual orientation", naming age explicitly ([Recital 58](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/recital/58/)). **Article 10** requires data-governance practices including "examination in view of possible biases that are likely to affect the health and safety of persons, have a negative impact on fundamental rights or lead to discrimination prohibited under Union law, especially where data outputs influence inputs for future operations" (10(2)(f)), "appropriate measures to detect, prevent and mitigate possible biases" (10(2)(g)), and data that are "relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete in view of the intended purpose" (10(3)) ([AI Act, Art. 10](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-10); [consolidated text](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/ai-act/ai-act-regulation/article-10/)). In taxonomy terms, 10(3)'s "free of errors" is a requirement about measurement bias including label error, so the thesis's injection can be framed as testing robustness to exactly the defect Article 10(3) asks providers to minimise but cannot verify. The **Digital Omnibus** postponed Annex III obligations to **2 December 2027** (Art. 113(c)(i)) and relocated the sensitive-data exception to Article 4a ([AI Act Explorer, Digital Omnibus](https://www.artificialintelligenceact.eu/ai-act-explorer/digital-omnibus/)) [verify against OJ]. The **Consumer Credit Directive 2** (Directive (EU) 2023/2225) applies from 20 November 2026; creditworthiness assessment must rely on "relevant and accurate information on the consumer's income and expenses", need not include GDPR special categories, excludes social networks as a source, and gives consumers a right to human intervention where assessment "involves automated processing" ([EP Legislative Observatory](https://oeil.europarl.europa.eu/oeil/en/document-summary?id=1764052)); its Article 6 non-discrimination clause referencing Charter Article 21, whose grounds include age, and its Article 18 explanation rights could not be fetched and should be quoted from the OJ [verify].

In the **United States**, ECOA and Regulation B list age "(provided the applicant has the capacity to contract)" among prohibited bases, yet 12 CFR 1002.6(b)(2) permits age as a predictor in an "empirically derived, demonstrably and statistically sound" system "provided that the age of an elderly applicant is not assigned a negative factor", with applicants aged 62 or over to be treated at least as favourably as those under 62 ([FDIC Compliance Manual V-7](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-compliance-examination-manual/v-7-equal-credit-opportunity-act-ecoa)); the young group the thesis studies therefore has no special US statutory protection. On 13 November 2025 the CFPB proposed removing the Regulation B provisions supporting disparate-impact liability, on its "preliminary view that disparate-impact claims are not available under ECOA" ([Orrick InfoBytes, 2025](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2025-11-14/cfpb-proposes-changes-to-regulation-b-on-disparate-impact-and-other-provisions/)), which makes disparate *treatment*, i.e. explicit use of the attribute at inference, the binding US constraint on group-conditional calibration. In **Switzerland**, Article 8(2) of the Federal Constitution states that "No person must be discriminated against due to their age", but this binds the state, "Swiss private law does not provide for any particular anti-age discrimination legislation", and the available source discusses employment only ([agediscrimination.info](https://www.agediscrimination.info/international-age-discrimination/switzerland)); a 2026 VASOS/FARES petition demands federal legislation covering private-law relations but does not mention banks or loans ([VASOS petition](https://syndicom.ch/app/uploads/2026/06/VASOS-Petition-Discrimation-liee-a-lage.pdf)). No Swiss source on credit-specific protections (KKG creditworthiness checks, revised DSG Article 21 on automated decisions, FINMA guidance) was retrieved, so the thesis should frame fairness by age in Swiss credit as an ethical and emerging-policy matter and must not assert a Swiss legal prohibition. Germany's AGG, which lists age, and AlgorithmWatch's finding that bureaus "legen die Funktionsweise ihres Credit Scorings allerdings nicht offen" are the nearest German-language comparators ([AlgorithmWatch, 2022](https://algorithmwatch.org/de/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/HUB_Draft_Policy_Brief_5_v3.pdf)). The synthesis is that the EU is moving towards *procedural* obligations (bias examination, data governance, human oversight, human intervention) rather than a single statistical criterion, onto which a per-group reliability guarantee maps naturally: abstention or a {good, bad} set is a trigger for the human review that CCD2 and AI Act Article 14 require.

### 2.3.4 Data limitations the chapter must carry forward

Four limitations recur. Protected attributes are usually unavailable or legally unusable, so studies rely on gender and age in public data and on merges or proxies in US mortgage data; Valdrighi et al. exclude gender "because of legal restrictions in many jurisdictions" and list "handling unknown sensitive attributes" as future work ([Valdrighi et al., 2024](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2409.20536)). Legitimate features act as proxies, so removing the attribute does not remove disparity (Hurlin et al.; Fuster et al.; Zandee's 80 % recoverability). Public benchmarks are small, old and dominant. All are accepted-only samples, so coverage guarantees hold for the *accepted* population; tested reject-inference methods "did not significantly improve AUC" ([Valdrighi et al., 2024](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2409.20536)). For the thesis specifically: German Credit's n = 1,000 and GMSC's 2 % sensitive-group share limit the precision of group-conditional coverage estimates; GMSC lacks gender, so only age can be studied there; and the Statlog code table is wrong for several attributes, which is why Grömping's South German Credit should be used, with the caveat that "sex cannot be fully recovered" because one family-status level merges female non-singles with male singles ([UCI 522](https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/522/south+german+credit)). Label definitions differ across benchmarks (90 days past due, charge-off, "serious delinquency"), and no source systematically compares their effect on fairness metrics; this heterogeneity is itself an argument that the "default" label is a lender-defined construct open to differential measurement.

## 2.4 Conformal prediction guarantees validity marginally; fairness asks for it per group

*Suggested heading: "Uncertainty quantification and conformal prediction."*

### 2.4.1 Why point predictions and calibrated probabilities are not enough

Modern discriminative models are "poorly calibrated", and temperature scaling, "a single-parameter variant of Platt scaling", is effective at repairing average calibration ([Guo et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599)); but post-hoc calibration gives no per-instance or finite-sample guarantee. Conformal prediction is the family that does: its sets are valid "in a distribution-free sense: they possess explicit, non-asymptotic guarantees" with no distributional or model assumptions ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)), under exchangeability, "a relaxed assumption of i.i.d." that "does not imply independence" ([Zhou et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.06494v3)). The RAPS paper documents the practical interaction: softmax probabilities "are known to be incorrect", so naive sets "miss the target coverage", tail probabilities are "often badly miscalibrated" and inflate set sizes, yet "the conformal threshold corrects miscalibration empirically, so APS and RAPS reach coverage regardless of model and dataset" ([Angelopoulos et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14193)). Bayesian neural networks, deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) and MC dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) should be cited from the originals as the model-dependent alternatives [verify]; Lu et al. (2022) compare CP with epistemic-uncertainty measures in a clinical setting ([Lu et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04392)). For credit the pitch is that a PD of 0.07 says nothing about how often it is wrong for *this* applicant, that calibration makes the score honest on average, and that CP converts any score into a set with a guaranteed error rate; the bridge to fairness is that marginal coverage and average calibration share the same blind spot, namely that a protected subgroup can be systematically under-covered while the population average is met.

### 2.4.2 Split conformal foundations

The chapter should state the guarantee with both bounds. With q̂ the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of calibration nonconformity scores, 1−α ≤ P(Y_test ∈ C(X_test)) ≤ 1−α + 1/(n+1), valid "no matter what (possibly incorrect) model is used or what the (unknown) distribution of the data is", with the upper bound requiring continuous scores ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)); Lei et al. (2018) give the split-conformal Theorem 2.2 and the full-conformal Theorem 2.1, note that full conformal "refits the regression for each trial y and each query point", and report that efficient full conformal in high dimensions is "still an open problem" ([Lei et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.04173)). Historical attribution runs from Vovk, Gammerman and collaborators (1999, 2005) through Papadopoulos et al. (2002) for the inductive variant to Shafer & Vovk's 2008 tutorial ([Aldirawi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01502), reference list). The result the thesis will use most is the **Beta law**: conditional on the calibration set, coverage is Beta(n+1−l, l) with l = ⌊(n+1)α⌋, so "n ≈ 1000 typically gives coverage between about .88 and .92", and the Gentle Introduction's Table 1 shows that **n ≈ 102 is needed for ±5 pp slack and 2,491 for ±1 pp** at δ = 0.1 ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). This is the right tool for explaining run-to-run variability with the few-hundred-point calibration sets South German Credit affords: at n = 200 and α = 0.1, Beta(181, 20) spreads several percentage points, so a per-group coverage of 0.87 is not by itself a fairness failure. Exact coverage with discrete scores needs randomised tie-breaking; the deterministic variant is "slightly conservative" ([Angelopoulos et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14193)), which matters for tree ensembles and saturated networks on tabular credit data.

### 2.4.3 Conditional coverage is impossible, group-conditional coverage is cheap but not free

Distribution-free coverage conditional on X = x is unattainable: Foygel Barber et al.'s Proposition 1 shows that any method achieving it has infinite expected set length at almost every non-atomic point, and their Theorem 2 shows that even the relaxed (1−α, δ) version forces lengths "achieved by the trivial marginal-coverage construction" ([Foygel Barber et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.04684)); Lei et al.'s Remark 2.3 makes the same point for intervals, and the Gentle Introduction illustrates it with two groups of 90 % and 10 % of the population where a method can have 90 % marginal coverage "while covering nobody in the smaller group" ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). The intuition for the chapter: conditioning on a continuous X is conditioning on a probability-zero event that no finite calibration sample can observe, whereas conditioning on a group with P(X ∈ G) = p_g leaves an effective calibration size of about n·p_g, so the ordinary split-conformal argument applies within the group. Group-conditional coverage over a finite partition is therefore achievable exactly by per-group calibration (Foygel Barber et al., Eq. 7, attributed to Vovk, 2012 and Lei & Wasserman, 2014), and the cost is explicit in three forms: Romano et al.'s Theorem 1 gives an upper bound of **1−α + 1/(|I₂(a)|+1)** per group ([Romano et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.05428v1)); Vadlamani et al.'s Lemma 3.1 gives **[1−α, 1−α + 1/(|D_calib(g,ỹ)|+1)]** for a (group, label) cell ([Vadlamani et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.16115v2)); and Gibbs, Cherian & Candès's Corollary 1 gives, for a finite collection of possibly overlapping groups, **≤ 1−α + |𝒢|/((n+1)·P(X ∈ G))**, with a quantile-regression construction that reformulates conditional coverage "as coverage over a class of covariate shifts" and a "spectrum between marginal and conditional validity" ([Gibbs et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12616)). Jung et al.'s multivalid coverage adds threshold-conditionality for overlapping groups ([Jung et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15145)). The practical consequence is the one the experimental-design review of this project drew out: every guarantee is a per-cell guarantee, intersectional attributes multiply cells and shrink n_g geometrically, and this is precisely Zhou & Sesia's rationale for AFCP, since exhaustive equalized coverage over all attributes "necessitates splitting the data into exponentially many subsets, significantly reducing the effective sample size" ([Zhou & Sesia, 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15106v2)).

### 2.4.4 Classification scores

Sadinle, Lei & Wasserman define set-valued classifiers that "meet a user-defined coverage level while keeping the output set small", show the optimal rule comes from level sets of the class-conditional probability, and note it "can sometimes output the empty set" ([Sadinle et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.00451)); the "1−p̂" naming and the label-conditional variant are in the full text, not verified here. APS cumulates sorted softmax mass down to the true label, which the Gentle Introduction credits with avoiding the under-coverage of hard subgroups seen with the naive method ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)); the original (Romano, Sesia & Candès, 2020, NeurIPS) was not fetched [verify]. RAPS adds a rank penalty λ(o_x(y) − k_reg)₊ because APS "is sensitive to noisy probability estimates far down the ranked list", cutting ImageNet ResNet-152 set sizes from **10.4 (APS) to 2.11 (RAPS) at 90 % coverage** and halving size-stratified coverage violation ([Angelopoulos et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.14193)). Two inferences are specific to binary credit scoring. First, with K = 2 the APS score for the true label is either p̂(y) or 1, so without randomisation the set jumps from {ŷ} to {0, 1} and set size is a thresholded function of max-probability; randomised APS (the U·π̂ term) is what makes exact coverage attainable with such saturated scores, and RAPS's rank penalty is moot for K = 2. Second, with an over-confident classifier the score distribution is highly discrete, the finite-sample quantile sits on a tie, and realised coverage can be noticeably above target, which is a concrete reason to report singleton and empty-set rates alongside average set size. Class-conditional (Mondrian) CP calibrates within each class ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)); Ding et al. note that per-class methods "do not work well when there is a limited amount of labeled data per class" and propose clustered CP ([Ding et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09335)). The design review established that the AFCP code defaults to label-conditional calibration (`conditional=True`) although the paper's main text presents marginal sets, so for the thesis the (group × label) cell is the unit of analysis and marginal versus label-conditional calibration is a primary experimental factor rather than a nuisance flag ([AFCP repository](https://github.com/FionaZ3696/Adaptively-Fair-Conformal-Prediction)).

### 2.4.5 Fairness-aware CP methods and the informativeness critique

**Equalized coverage** (Romano, Barber, Sabatti & Candès, 2020) requires P{Y ∈ C(X, A) | A = a} ≥ 1−α for every a, implemented by calibrating the (1−α)(1+1/|I₂(a)|) quantile within each group, with "groupwise" and "joint" training variants; on MEPS the fitted model overestimated utilisation for non-white individuals, marginal CP covered **0.920 non-white versus 0.871 white**, all conditional variants hit ≈0.90 per group, and intervals were wider for the white group; the authors state that differing lengths "should be investigated rather than hidden" and that not using protected attributes "is certainly not sufficient to guarantee absence of discrimination" ([Romano et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.05428v1)). Its HDSR venue is from search listings, not a fetched page [verify]. **Lu et al. (2022)** adopt the same notion for dermatology by skin tone with group-conditional APS/RAPS ([Lu et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04392)), AAAI venue unverified. **AFCP** (Zhou & Sesia, 2024) selects, for each test point, "at most one sensitive attribute, using a leave-one-out procedure with a placeholder label for each class", targeting "the group most negatively affected by algorithmic bias", builds the set as the union of the marginal set and the label-wise group-conditional set so that marginal validity is retained, and proves adaptive equalized coverage conditional on the selected attribute via "an innovative proof technique" handling the non-exchangeability that selection induces; exhaustive sets are "valid but conservative", partial sets "often still too conservative", and the authors concede the method "does not always identify the most relevant sensitive attribute, particularly when working with limited sample sizes" ([Zhou & Sesia, 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15106v2); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10625837)). Its experiments use synthetic medical data, UCI Nursery, COMPAS and Adult, **never credit**, and its selection test is a one-sided t-test the authors call "heuristic". Zhou & Sesia explicitly decline to commit to a classical fairness metric, calling fairness "partly subjective". **EOC** (Wang et al., 2023) argues that equalized coverage "does not guarantee equal coverage across finer-grained groups conditioned on the true label" and requires coverage parity among groups with similar outcomes while optimising interval width ([Wang et al., 2023](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/1849b94ed817ae7043a6b6934ef410c1-Abstract.html)). **Conformal Fairness** (Vadlamani et al., 2025) bounds coverage gaps within a tolerance c per positive label, implements a four-fifths disparate-impact rule (1−α_max)/(1−α_min) ≥ 0.8, searches the threshold λ over a grid, and needs no group label at inference; on ACSIncome it raised DI from 0.397 to 0.815, and against BatchGCP (DI 0.349) it reached 0.797 ([Vadlamani et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.16115v2)). Newer items surfaced but were not fetched: FedCF (arXiv:2509.22907), counterfactually fair CP ([Guldogan et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08724)), representation-based-group fair CP (arXiv:2605.12195).

The **informativeness critique** is the key limitation to state. Cresswell, Kumar, Sui & Belbahri (2025) argue that "coverage is not an outcome – it is a byproduct of how CP quantifies uncertainty", that "prediction sets are not inherently actionable", and that "providing sets that satisfy Equalized Coverage actually increases disparate impact compared to marginal coverage"; in pre-registered experiments with **600 Prolific participants** across image, text and audio tasks, Mondrian (equalized-coverage) sets "produced the largest disparate impact" (FACET max odds ratio 1.51 versus 1.26 marginal; RAVDESS 1.28 versus 1.01), set-size disparity was far larger under conditional CP (FACET Δ_Size 3.16 versus 0.75), and "it is set size, not coverage, which has the greatest influence on human accuracy", leading them to propose equalized set size or singleton frequency as alternatives, given in prose and not tested as treatments ([Cresswell et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01888v2); [ICLR proceedings](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/32347ceb5729d6f50c1b16b43a8d5441-Paper-Conference.pdf)). The 2026 follow-up distinguishes procedural from substantive fairness, decomposes set-size disparity into interpretable components, and reports that "equalized set sizes, rather than equalized coverage, correlate strongly with better substantive fairness" ([Liu et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16794)). The chapter should present a three-way taxonomy, coverage-parity approaches (Romano; Lu; AFCP; Gibbs; Jung; Vadlamani), informativeness-parity critiques (Cresswell; Liu), and causal notions (Guldogan), place the thesis in the first with the second's metrics reported, and translate the human-in-the-loop argument to underwriting: a {good, bad} set for a protected group is "no information" for that group, so equalized coverage achieved through larger sets can shift the burden of manual review onto exactly the group the intervention was meant to protect. Two structural observations tie this back to §2.2: the sample-size cost and the disparate-impact cost are two faces of one trade-off, since harder groups need larger sets to reach the same coverage; and fair CP moves the fairness trade-off from a *correctness* axis (accuracy, profit) to an *informativeness* axis (set size, singleton rate), which is why Cresswell's concern is the CP counterpart of the accuracy cost in classical fairness. Note for citation hygiene that the Zhou et al. (2025) ACM CSUR survey "Conformal prediction: a data perspective" contains **no dedicated treatment of fairness**; it should be cited only for its data-centric taxonomy and conditional-coverage overview ([Zhou et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.06494v3)). Uncertainty-and-fairness benchmarks exist but do not use CP: FairlyUncertain finds that abstaining "reduces error … but does not reduce outcome imbalances between demographic groups" ([Rosenblatt & Witter, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02005)), and Panero, Wit & Scutari argue that fairness assessments should quantify model-selection uncertainty ([Panero et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.07959)); no dedicated survey of conformal or Bayesian fairness was found.

### 2.4.6 CP under label noise and distribution shift

Einbinder et al. (2022) study calibration labels Ỹ_i = g(Y_i) corrupted while test labels stay clean and prove that "naïve conformal prediction covers the noiseless ground truth label unless the noise distribution is adversarially designed": APS with any ranking-consistent classifier is valid under random-flip noise (Prop. 1), HPS when ε ≤ (1−1/K)² (Prop. 2), the mechanism being that noise which spreads out the score distribution makes the noisy quantile stochastically dominate the clean one. The negative results are the ones that matter here: Proposition 5 shows that for any nontrivial noise some score under-covers, Corollary 1 recommends running at α′ = α + (n/(n+1))ε when the total-variation noise size ε is known, Proposition 6 shows no general TV bound exists, and empirically **rare-to-frequent, biased and adversarial noise lowers coverage to roughly 85–89 %** while uniform 5 % flips raise it to ≈94 % with larger sets; the paper "does not discuss group-dependent noise or fairness" ([Einbinder et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.14295)). Barber et al. (2023) give the general non-exchangeable bound, P{Y ∈ C} ≥ 1−α − Σ w̃_i·d_TV(Z, Z^i), with fixed weights that guard against unknown small violations and recover exact coverage under exchangeability ([Barber et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2202.13415)); Tibshirani et al.'s covariate-shift reweighting is cited via the Gentle Introduction's Theorem 3 ([Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). The theoretical prediction the thesis can test follows directly: group-dependent, one-sided flipping shifts the targeted group's score distribution asymmetrically rather than spreading it, so it falls outside Einbinder's positive results; **coverage of the *clean* label should drop for that group while marginal coverage over the *noisy* labels remains exactly valid**, with the loss bounded by order ε_g through Barber et al.'s TV term. A limitation to state explicitly: group-conditional calibration on noisy labels equalises coverage of the *noisy* label, not of true creditworthiness. No paper was found that studies group-dependent label noise in conformal calibration.

### 2.4.7 Conformal risk control as the extension point

Conformal risk control "extend[s] conformal prediction to control the expected value of any monotone loss function", choosing λ̂ = inf{λ : (n/(n+1))R̂_n(λ) + B/(n+1) ≤ α} and reducing to split CP for the miscoverage loss ([Angelopoulos et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814); [Angelopoulos & Bates, 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.07511)). The Project's uploaded paper, Aldirawi, Li & Guo (2026), removes the monotonicity requirement for a finite grid of M thresholds, proving excess risk **ε(n, M) = B√(log(2M)/(2n)) + B/(2√(2n log(2M)))** with a matching minimax lower bound of order √(log M / n), and tabulating ε ≈ 0.056 at n = 1,000 and M = 100, 0.025 at n = 5,000 and 0.018 at n = 10,000, so that "the inflation rate ε/α becomes small once the calibration sample reaches a few thousand observations"; it explicitly lists "fairness-related metrics in algorithmic decision-making" among the risk functionals one may wish to control, and its Proposition 4 extends the result to importance-weighted distribution shift ([Aldirawi et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01502)). The relation to the thesis is clean: per-group coverage is monotone in the threshold, so split CP and CRC apply exactly, but the quantities Cresswell and Liu argue actually matter, set-size disparity, singleton-rate gap, or a combined miscoverage-plus-oversize penalty (the ImageNet loss in Aldirawi et al. §5.2), are *non-monotone* in λ. A natural extension is to define a group-fairness loss on a λ-grid (for instance the maximum over groups of |miscoverage_g − α| plus a weighted set-size gap, or Vadlamani's four-fifths violation) and apply CRC-NM, obtaining a finite-sample guarantee on the fairness–informativeness trade-off rather than on coverage alone, with the practical message that a few thousand calibration points suffice for M ≈ 100–200 thresholds; on the thesis's data that is feasible for Taiwan and GMSC but not for South German Credit.

## 2.6 How this thesis positions itself

The four literatures reviewed above meet at a point none of them occupies. Credit-fairness research measures *decision* and *error-rate* disparities and has concluded that they originate mainly in data; it has never measured whether the *reliability* of a model's uncertainty differs by group, and its only conformal applications are marginal-coverage exercises without a fairness lens. Fairness-aware conformal prediction has produced finite-sample methods, equalized coverage, AFCP, EOC and Conformal Fairness, validated on health, census, image, text and audio data, and has been met by a credible critique that equalizing coverage can worsen downstream disparate impact through set size. Bias-taxonomy and bias-injection research has identified group-dependent label bias as measurement bias on the target, has shown it to be the mechanism that defeats Y-conditional metrics and post-processing, and has established it as the manipulation that produces clean dose–response curves, but has never crossed it with conformal calibration, and conformal label-noise theory covers symmetric noise with positive results while leaving one-sided, group-dependent noise as the regime where the guarantee is expected to fail. The regulatory frame, meanwhile, now asks for exactly what the intersection would deliver: Article 10 of the AI Act demands examination of biases "especially where data outputs influence inputs for future operations" and data "free of errors", CCD2 and Article 14 require human intervention in automated creditworthiness assessment, and the ACPR asks for uncertainty around fairness metrics.

The thesis's contribution should be stated in these terms. It compares marginal CP, partial and exhaustive equalized coverage and AFCP on three public credit datasets, South German Credit, Give Me Some Credit and UCI Taiwan, measuring per-group and per-(group × label) coverage, set size, singleton and empty-set rates, and AFCP's attribute-selection frequency, under both marginal and label-conditional calibration regimes; and it injects group-dependent one-sided label bias in the disadvantaged group, "stigma" (good→bad) as the primary mechanism grounded in Baumann et al.'s measurement bias on Y and "hidden defaults" (bad→good) as the symmetric robustness check grounded in Fogliato et al.'s hidden-positives model, into training and calibration data while keeping test labels clean, following Einbinder et al.'s protocol, with a strength grid that starts at 0.05 because Fogliato et al. show that audit conclusions already flip at α ≈ 0.03–0.07. The design review that precedes this report fixed the operational details that the chapter need not repeat: cell-size-driven split design, early-stopped classifiers with degeneracy checks, explicit reporting of the calibration regime, and paired-by-seed tests. What the Background chapter must do is make the hypotheses falsifiable from the literature: under stigma flips, marginal CP should lose clean-label coverage on the targeted group's defaulters first; equalized-coverage methods should restore coverage at the price of the set-size and singleton gaps Cresswell et al. warn about; and AFCP's selection frequency should rise with the flip rate only once the (group × label) cell is large enough for its test to detect it, which the Beta and 1/(n_g+1) bounds predict will happen on Taiwan before GMSC and never on South German Credit. The gap is therefore not merely "no one has done this on credit data"; it is that the one bias mechanism the fairness literature identifies as hardest, applied to the one guarantee the CP literature identifies as fragile, in the one domain where the law now requires both bias examination and per-applicant human oversight, has not been studied, and that the thesis's semi-synthetic design is the only way to study it against a clean label that real lending data cannot supply.
